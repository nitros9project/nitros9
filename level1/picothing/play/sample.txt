The Lighthouse Keeper's Machine

On a small island at the edge of the map there stood a lighthouse, and
in the lighthouse lived a keeper who loved machines. Every evening,
when the lamp was lit and the tide had turned, she climbed the narrow
stair to a room full of wire and solder and patient, humming boxes.

Her favourite was a little computer with an eight-bit heart. It could
not do very much at once, but it did everything carefully, one step at
a time, and it never forgot what it had been asked. She taught it to
count the waves. She taught it to keep the log of passing ships. On
stormy nights she taught it to play a quiet game of chess, and it lost
gracefully, which she thought was the mark of a good companion.

The machine spoke through a row of blinking lights and a screen of
green letters. When she typed, it answered. When she asked it to read
a long story, it read every word and told her how many there had been,
which letters it had met most often, and which word was the longest.
She liked that. It was like asking a friend what they had noticed.

One winter the supply boat was late, and the keeper grew lonely. She
wrote a program that would greet her each morning with a line of
verse, chosen from a list she had typed in by hand. Some mornings the
verse was cheerful, and some mornings it was sad, and once, by an
error she never quite found, it printed the same line forty times in
a row. She laughed until the gulls on the railing took fright.

In spring the boat came back with flour and oil and letters from the
mainland. The captain asked her how she had passed the long dark
months alone. She thought about the humming boxes, the green letters,
the chess games lost with such good grace, and the forty identical
lines of verse.

"I was never really alone," she said. "I had the lamp to tend, the
sea to watch, and a machine that listened. What more could anyone
want?"

The captain did not understand, but he nodded politely, and when he
sailed away that evening the light swept out across the water behind
him, steady and bright, counted and logged by a small machine that
had never once lost track of the waves.
